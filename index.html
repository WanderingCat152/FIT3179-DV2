Access depends on where you live

How evenly are essential services located near residents across Greater Melbourne, and where does being farther from services overlap with greater community need?

01

Access depends on where you live

Nearby access drops sharply toward several outer local government areas (LGAs). LGAs are council areas used here to compare communities. The overall score varies by more than 63 points across the study area; the maps below establish that gap and show the facilities behind it.

Overall access to nearby facilities

This project-created score gives equal importance to four service categories. It estimates straight-line proximity and is not an official accessibility index. Melbourne and Cardinia are labelled as the highest and lowest results; hover over any LGA for its detailed values.

Reading the map: darker areas indicate a larger estimated share of residents living in neighbourhoods whose representative point is within 800 metres in a straight line of a mapped facility. This is not walking distance.

Where facilities concentrate

Select one category at a time. Each shape is an approximate 5 km grid cell; darker cells contain more mapped facilities.

Why aggregate the points? The grid makes concentration visible without allowing hundreds of coincident symbols to hide one another. Coastal cells are clipped to the study boundary. Counts are not adjusted for population, capacity or service quality.

03

When low access meets greater need

Lower access sometimes overlaps with greater community need—but not everywhere. Relative disadvantage is measured with the Australian Bureau of Statistics Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage (IRSD): a lower IRSD score means greater disadvantage. IRSD and households without a motor vehicle are shown as context, not included in the project-created access score or treated as proof of causation.

Where living without a car is most common

Each line starts at a representative point for an LGA. Line length represents the share of dwellings without a motor vehicle, reducing the overlap created by proportional circles.

Car-free households concentrate in well-connected inner LGAs, especially Melbourne and Yarra. The line-symbol map provides community context; it is not itself an access score.

Four LGAs combine lower access with higher disadvantage

Both measures are divided into three roughly equal groups across metropolitan Melbourne. Dark purple identifies lower access combined with higher relative disadvantage. The disadvantage groups reverse the IRSD scale, because a lower IRSD score means greater disadvantage; original values remain in the tooltip.

Casey, Wyndham, Frankston and Brimbank fall in the lower-access, higher-disadvantage group under these relative metropolitan thresholds.

Access rises across car-free household groups

LGAs are divided into three near-equal groups by their no-car household share. Points show overall access, colour shows relative disadvantage based on IRSD, and the black tick marks the middle value for each group.

Median access rises from 32.6 in the lower no-car group to 42.3 in the middle group and 60.0 in the higher group. This is an association, not evidence that low car ownership causes access.

From disadvantage to access and the weakest service

Read from left to right: the flow starts with relative disadvantage based on IRSD, passes through overall access, and ends at each LGA's weakest service type. Wider ribbons represent more residents in the 2021 population.

Cultural facilities are the weakest service type for LGAs containing 73.8% of the study population. The flow also shows that disadvantage and low access overlap, but not in every community.

05

What the measure can and cannot say

These charts estimate proximity, not service quality. The final comparison returns to the four service types, then separates supported findings from claims the measure cannot make.

Education is consistently closer than cultural facilities

Each box summarises the middle half of results across LGAs. Individual points show each LGA and remain available for inspection in the tooltip.

Median education coverage is 79.5%, compared with 15.9% for cultural facilities. The difference is visible across most of the metropolitan distribution, not only in one or two outlying LGAs.

What the evidence supports

Nearby access varies by place and service type

  • Overall access varies by more than 63 points across the 31 LGAs.
  • Education is generally the most geographically widespread service type.
  • Cultural or health facilities have the lowest coverage in every LGA.
  • Some lower-access LGAs also face higher relative disadvantage.

What it cannot establish

Nearby does not necessarily mean usable

  • Straight-line distance is not a measured walking route or travel time.
  • Facility points do not describe capacity, quality, cost or opening hours.
  • LGA averages can conceal variation between neighbourhoods.
  • The sources come from different years, so this is a snapshot rather than evidence of historical change.

Method summary

How the access estimate works

A transparent measure of straight-line closeness designed for comparison—not an official government accessibility index.

  1. 1
    Represent each neighbourhood

    Use one ABS representative point for each small Census neighbourhood, known as a Statistical Area Level 1 (SA1).

  2. 2
    Test nearby access

    Check whether the nearest mapped facility is within 800 m in a straight line—not by an actual walking route.

  3. 3
    Summarise by LGA

    Give more influence to SA1 neighbourhoods with more residents in 2021, then take an equal average across the four service types.
